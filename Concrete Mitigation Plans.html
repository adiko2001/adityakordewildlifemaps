From Spatial Hazard Models to Concrete Mitigation Plans

Converting conflict polygons, slope thresholds, and fire perimeters into actionable, budget-conscious industrial access solutions.

1. Strategic Access Decommissioning (The 558.8 km Priority Target):

Instead of leaving old logging tracks open to the public, the model isolated inactive and abandoned roads on slopes steeper than 15° while leaving active timber hauling routes alone.

The restoration action: Bringing in heavy machinery to dig up the hard-packed dirt roadbeds and block vehicle access. Ripping up the compacted soil lets water soak naturally into the mountainside (stopping erosion and mudslides) while permanently cutting off pickup trucks, poachers, and ATVs from driving straight into grizzly feeding areas.

2. Mitigating Risk in Narrow Movement Corridors

Wildfires force wide-ranging carnivores out of burned woods and push them into the surviving forest left behind. By calculating a perimeter-to-area constriction metric across Alberta's wildfire perimeters, the model mapped long, thin strips of forest where animals have very little room to travel. When these narrow routes cross active highways and resource roads, displaced bears face heavy vehicle traffic and higher collision risks. Land managers can use these mapped collision zones to target practical fixes on the ground—such as seasonal driver warning signs, lower speed limits, and specific spots to build wildlife crossing overpasses or underpasses.

3. Managing Road Access Instead of Banning Forestry

Over 90% of prime grizzly bear feeding habitat in southeastern British Columbia sits on working Crown land outside protected parks. Banning commercial logging across these valleys is completely unrealistic because forestry drives the local economy, and early-seral clearings naturally produce the berry patches bears rely on. The practical solution is managing human access instead of fighting the harvest. Timber companies can harvest forest cutblocks and haul logs along active roads, but once an operating area is finished, the access routes should be permanently shut down. Digging up the compacted dirt on inactive roads keeps active hauling routes running, supports local timber jobs, and ensures feeding clearings do not remain open for vehicles, hunters, and poachers.
